I would like to request the use and storage of Copper electroplating solution at SNF (attached Process/Materials Form).

1.  Reason for request: Use in flexible electronic systems as low stress and high conductivity interconnects. Desired plated thickness is 5um.

1.  Protocol of usage: Will be used in the fume hood of PDMS room 155A (discussed with Michelle)

*   Plating solution will be dispensed into a 2000 mL pyrex breaker
*   Solution will be filled to the 1500 mL mark
*   Beaker will be placed onto the magnetic stirrer available in the room 155A in the fumehood.
*   Power supply: (30 V max; 5 A max) --> my usage would be up to 1A of current. https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B00ZBCLJSY/ref=ox_sc_act_title_11?smid=AMH4W1K8OCGMX&psc=1
*   2 copper anodes will be used
*   4 inch wafer will be suspended in the beaker with the plating solution using a rod and clamps
*   24 gauge copper wires will be used for connections to the main lead connections for the power supply
*   Room temperature process only. No heating will be required.
*   Another beaker with filled with DI water will be used for cleaning of the wafer. Then drying with N2.

Note: Appropriate protective gear like gloves and apron will be used

1.  Process (Disposal):

*   Once finished with the process, plating solution will be dispensed into a ?waste? container that will be labeled by my name and with a tag of the solution. This solution will be reused over and over until no longer possible. Then it will be taken to the waste collection area of other hazardous solutions in SNF.
*   Gloves and wipes contaminated with the plating solution will be placed in a plastic Ziploc and thrown in the hazardous waste bin.
*   For small spills, absorb on rags or cleanroom wipes and dispose a
*   Cont Teflon coated metal tweezers will be used for this process

Disposal and protection practices are listed in details in the attached MSDS.

1.  Amount and form: 1 gallon poly bottle, meeting UN requirements.

1.  Storage:  Store in a cool, dry area. Use with adequate ventilation. Keep container tightly closed when not in use. Store only in the original container. Protect from physical damage. Empty containers may contain hazardous residue.

*   Storage Group: G: Not Intrinsically Reactive or Flammable or Combustible
*   Health: 3 Flammability: 0 Reactivity: 0 Personal Protection: C

1.  Disposal:

Spill, Leak or Release: Isolate spill area and avoid tracking through liquid. Dike and prevent runoff to drains or sewers. For small spills, cover with lime or soda ash and then scoop into polyethylene drums for later disposal. Large spill may be pumped directly into a storage container for later disposal. Do not wash residue to drain or sewer. Copper sulfate solution may deteriorate concrete. Refer to Section 15 for spill/release reporting information.

Please let me know if you want to discuss this further in person and if any additional information is required.

Hi all -

I've a couple of concerns...

We haven't typically allowed nanoparticles and nanoparticle solutions
(except for latex, which cleans up easily) to be spun up in the headway2
or, indeed, in the cleanroom.? The reason is that when you spin coat a
wafer, the resist spinning off the edges becomes an aerosol.? These
droplets can dry quickly and end up getting blown around the bench
because of the high exhaust pulling up.? At least this is my
thinking...? We don't have any confirmed data -- but James Conway would
intermittently find nanodots on his samples which shouldn't have any...
I used to work in a lab where the goal was to get aerosolized drugs into
the lungs of mice and rats -- we had all sorts of precautions about
making sure these aerosolized drugs didn't get into our lungs, because
there's all sorts of data about how easy this is, so I had good reason
to believe James on this.

So, while I'm sure that the exhaust in the Headway2 bench is sufficient
to prevent exposure to users, I do believe that spin coating can
aerosolize particles.? And because the bench is plastic and there's lots
of air flow, the surfaces are an electrostatic magnet for particles.?
This is why we've directed people to spin coaters in 155 for spin
coating solutions with nanoparticles and CNT's.

In this case, the solutions are resists, so would get exposed in 155.?
Chatting with Carsen, one possible solution would be the Headway3
bench.? This is metal, rather than plastic, so wouldn't have the
static-cling effect.? The headway3 also has better downward exhaust.? I
could be persuaded to think that if there are provisions to cover the
headway3 while spinning and perform a wipe-down of the station
afterwards, this might work.? That said, it might be easier to get a bug
light for 155 or 159.

As for developing...? InP waste should be collected locally (the SDS
says "do not let product enter drains").? This means the svgdev should
not be used since this waste goes to AWN and eventually the drains.? The
wet benches can be used for manual develop, but the developer waste and
rinsate should be collected locally and labeled for EH&S disposal.

Your thoughts?

I would suggest bringing up these concerns to David and maybe ask him to
consider contacting Nanosys to ask if they have recommendations for
safely handling their materials.? It's entirely possible they won't,
outside of the SDS.? But they might be helpful.

Mary

Hi all -

It's not entirely clear what Hansen's experiment is here, but I think he
may want to attend the next Process Clinic.? Presuming that he's
characterizing the eutectic process, I'm guessing he will want to
understand the temperature versus pressure versus bond time effects.?
And if I understand that correctly, then I think there are a few things
that would need to be addressed, which are not clear to me in this request:

1.? Connections and set up.? Some thought about the safety aspects of
the set up are needed.? For example, ensuring everything is secured
before turning on power. Maybe a way to limit current.? I worked in a
lab where we removed anything metallic from hands and heads, including
making sure we had plastic safety glasses, because it's so easy for a
wire to pop off and make contact with you or the metal tables and
equipment we worked on. Anyway, someone should go through the set up
with Hansen, both for this request and probably again when he brings his
power supply in.

2.? Resistive heating on silicon.? Depending on the layers and layout, I
think he could exceed the Si threshold voltage and push a lot of
electrons through the substrate.? I guess he'd just get more resistive
heating, so maybe that's OK.? But it seems better science to know this
going in.? It would be good to have an idea of the voltages and currents
involved, either through a back-of-the-envelope calculation or, better
yet, through making some samples and testing in his own work bench.

3. Temperature control.? It's extra work, but might be worth it to
pattern an extra metal layer with a vastly different Seebeck coefficient
to obtain a built-in thermocouple.? For gold (is he using gold?),
nickel, iron, or tungsten might be good candidates. He could put these
in different locations on the substrate or even the pyrex side to get an
idea of what temperature distribution is like.? I think the heat flow
will be weird on this and will depend on how big his silicon substrate
is (since it's the heat dissipator) and its thermal contact with the
chuck.? So, I could be wrong, but suspect that the reproducibility of
the experiment will depend on a lot factors, so the more systematic the
conditions and monitoring done, the better chance of controlled processing.
