Michelle,

I am not sure what Payton is trying to do.  My understanding was that he wanted to use "Laser Cutter" to get small pieces after the wafer is patterned and take the pieces back to processing in a "Clean" tool without any further clean.  But Payton has not stated this objective explicitly.  

He has not mentioned if he is going to clean the samples at all after laser cutting.  From the process flow, he is using the pliers to cleave the wafer.  Are we concerned about it?  Is there any possibility of metallic contamination from the pliers?  

I do not understand why he needs 3um SiO2 in thermco if he wants to look at line edge roughness during Si3N4 etch.  3um oxide will take forever to grow and he is not coating with oxide, but growing the oxide.  

If he is going to process metal contaminated samples, then we need to know what is the metal that may be exposed in the chamber and decide whether we allow it or not in a clean tool and if we do, whether any chamber clean is needed post etch.

I think we should ask for more explanation before we approve the request.

Usha

Hi all -

I've a couple of concerns...

We haven't typically allowed nanoparticles and nanoparticle solutions 
(except for latex, which cleans up easily) to be spun up in the headway2 
or, indeed, in the cleanroom.? The reason is that when you spin coat a 
wafer, the resist spinning off the edges becomes an aerosol.? These 
droplets can dry quickly and end up getting blown around the bench 
because of the high exhaust pulling up.? At least this is my 
thinking...? We don't have any confirmed data -- but James Conway would 
intermittently find nanodots on his samples which shouldn't have any... 
I used to work in a lab where the goal was to get aerosolized drugs into 
the lungs of mice and rats -- we had all sorts of precautions about 
making sure these aerosolized drugs didn't get into our lungs, because 
there's all sorts of data about how easy this is, so I had good reason 
to believe James on this.

So, while I'm sure that the exhaust in the Headway2 bench is sufficient 
to prevent exposure to users, I do believe that spin coating can 
aerosolize particles.? And because the bench is plastic and there's lots 
of air flow, the surfaces are an electrostatic magnet for particles.? 
This is why we've directed people to spin coaters in 155 for spin 
coating solutions with nanoparticles and CNT's.

In this case, the solutions are resists, so would get exposed in 155.? 
Chatting with Carsen, one possible solution would be the Headway3 
bench.? This is metal, rather than plastic, so wouldn't have the 
static-cling effect.? The headway3 also has better downward exhaust.? I 
could be persuaded to think that if there are provisions to cover the 
headway3 while spinning and perform a wipe-down of the station 
afterwards, this might work.? That said, it might be easier to get a bug 
light for 155 or 159.

As for developing...? InP waste should be collected locally (the SDS 
says "do not let product enter drains").? This means the svgdev should 
not be used since this waste goes to AWN and eventually the drains.? The 
wet benches can be used for manual develop, but the developer waste and 
rinsate should be collected locally and labeled for EH&S disposal.

Your thoughts?

I would suggest bringing up these concerns to David and maybe ask him to 
consider contacting Nanosys to ask if they have recommendations for 
safely handling their materials.? It's entirely possible they won't, 
outside of the SDS.? But they might be helpful.

Mary

Hi all -

I've a couple of concerns...

We haven't typically allowed nanoparticles and nanoparticle solutions (except for latex, which cleans up easily) to be spun up in the headway2 or, indeed, in the cleanroom.  The reason is that when you spin coat a wafer, the resist spinning off the edges becomes an aerosol.  These droplets can dry quickly and end up getting blown around the bench because of the high exhaust pulling up.  At least this is my thinking...  We don't have any confirmed data -- but James Conway would intermittently find nanodots on his samples which shouldn't have any... I used to work in a lab where the goal was to get aerosolized drugs into the lungs of mice and rats -- we had all sorts of precautions about making sure these aerosolized drugs didn't get into our lungs, because there's all sorts of data about how easy this is, so I had good reason to believe James on this.

So, while I'm sure that the exhaust in the Headway2 bench is sufficient to prevent exposure to users, I do believe that spin coating can aerosolize particles.  And because the bench is plastic and there's lots of air flow, the surfaces are an electrostatic magnet for particles.  This is why we've directed people to spin coaters in 155 for spin coating solutions with nanoparticles and CNT's.

In this case, the solutions are resists, so would get exposed in 155.  Chatting with Carsen, one possible solution would be the Headway3 bench.  This is metal, rather than plastic, so wouldn't have the static-cling effect.  The headway3 also has better downward exhaust.  I could be persuaded to think that if there are provisions to cover the headway3 while spinning and perform a wipe-down of the station afterwards, this might work.  That said, it might be easier to get a bug light for 155 or 159.

As for developing...  InP waste should be collected locally (the SDS says "do not let product enter drains").  This means the svgdev should not be used since this waste goes to AWN and eventually the drains.  The wet benches can be used for manual develop, but the developer waste and rinsate should be collected locally and labeled for EH&S disposal.

Your thoughts?

I would suggest bringing up these concerns to David and maybe ask him to consider contacting Nanosys to ask if they have recommendations for safely handling their materials.  It's entirely possible they won't, outside of the SDS.  But they might be helpful.

Mary
